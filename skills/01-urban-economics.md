# Urban Economics — Domain Skill

## Scope

This skill covers the study of cities as economic systems, with a focus on **residential real estate markets**. It provides the vocabulary, theoretical frameworks, and analytical standards used in urban economics to evaluate academic articles.

## Core Theoretical Frameworks

### Location Theory and Land Markets
- **Alonso-Muth-Mills monocentric city model**: residential location choice as a trade-off between commuting costs and housing consumption; bid-rent functions; density gradients.
- **Polycentric extensions**: sub-centers, edge cities (Garreau), polycentric urban structure (Anas, Arnott & Small).
- **Bid-rent theory**: how heterogeneous agents sort across space based on willingness to pay for location attributes.

### Hedonic Pricing
- **Rosen (1974) framework**: housing as a bundle of characteristics; hedonic price function as an envelope of bid and offer functions.
- **Identification challenges**: recovering structural demand/supply parameters from hedonic estimates (Ekeland, Heckman & Nesheim; Bajari & Benkard).
- **Key attributes**: structural (size, age, quality), locational (distance to CBD, transit access, school quality), neighborhood (crime, demographics, amenities), environmental (air quality, noise, green space, flood risk).

### Sorting and Tiebout
- **Tiebout (1956) model**: residential sorting by preferences for local public goods; "voting with feet."
- **Epple-Sieg sorting models**: equilibrium sorting with continuous heterogeneity in income and tastes.
- **Capitalization**: how local amenities and public goods are capitalized into house prices (Oates, Brueckner).

### Housing Supply
- **Glaeser-Gyourko regulatory constraints**: zoning, building permits, land use regulation as determinants of supply elasticity.
- **Saiz (2010) geographic/regulatory supply elasticity**: topographic constraints interacting with regulatory barriers.
- **Construction cost vs. land value decomposition**: distinguishing physical depreciation from land appreciation.

### Housing Demand and Affordability
- **User cost of housing**: mortgage rates, taxes, maintenance, expected appreciation.
- **Affordability indices**: price-to-income, rent-to-income, residual income approaches.
- **Tenure choice**: rent vs. own decision, down payment constraints, wealth effects of homeownership.

### Urban Dynamics
- **Gentrification**: neighborhood upgrading, displacement, filtering models (Rosenthal; Couture & Handbury).
- **Neighborhood change**: tipping models (Schelling, Card-Mas-Rothstein), racial transition, neighborhood life-cycle theory.
- **Housing cycles**: boom-bust dynamics, supply overhang, expectations and speculation (Glaeser, Gyourko & Saiz; Shiller).

## Vocabulary Guide

When summarizing articles in this domain, use these terms precisely:

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Capitalization | The extent to which a local amenity/disamenity is reflected in property values |
| Sorting | Self-selection of heterogeneous households into different locations based on preferences and budget |
| Supply elasticity | Responsiveness of new housing construction to price changes |
| Bid-rent gradient | The relationship between willingness to pay and distance from a central point |
| Hedonic regression | Statistical decomposition of price into implicit prices of housing characteristics |
| Willingness to pay (WTP) | Marginal valuation of an attribute, derived from hedonic or stated-preference methods |
| Amenity capitalization | How non-market goods (parks, schools, clean air) are priced into housing |
| Filtering | The process by which housing units transition between quality/income segments over time |
| Zoning tax | The implicit cost of land use regulation on housing prices (Glaeser & Gyourko) |
| Spatial equilibrium | A state where no household can improve utility by relocating, given prices and amenities |
|Extensive margin| The margin that is affected by a discrete change in the level to which an activity is undertaken. For example, moving from unemployment to working 40 hours per week is a change at the extensive margin |
|Intensive margin| The margin that is affected by changes in the level to which an existing activity is undertaken. For example, increasing work hours from 40 per week to 41 per week is a change at the intensive margin |

## What to Emphasize in Summaries

1. **Identification strategy**: How does the paper establish causality? What is the source of exogenous variation?
2. **Welfare implications**: What does the finding imply for household welfare, inequality, or efficiency?
3. **Policy relevance**: What regulatory or policy lever does the paper speak to (zoning reform, transit investment, rent control, housing subsidies)?
4. **Equilibrium reasoning**: Does the paper account for general equilibrium adjustments (e.g., migration responses, price adjustments)?
5. **Heterogeneity**: Does the paper examine differential effects across income groups, race/ethnicity, tenure status, or geography?

## Common Identification Strategies

- **Boundary discontinuity designs**: School district boundaries, municipal borders, zoning boundaries.
- **Natural experiments**: Plant closings, Superfund cleanups, highway construction, transit openings.
- **Instrumental variables**: Bartik instruments for labor demand, historical instruments (Saiz topography, colonial-era features).
- **Repeat-sales / panel methods**: Case-Shiller, within-property variation to control for unobserved quality.
- **Regression discontinuity**: Test score cutoffs for school assignment, income thresholds for program eligibility.
