# Geography — Domain Skill

## Scope

This skill covers **geographic approaches to residential real estate**, spanning quantitative spatial analysis, critical/qualitative geography, and the study of place, neighborhoods, and housing as a social and spatial phenomenon. Geography distinguishes itself from the other domains by its attention to **place specificity**, **multi-method inquiry**, and the **politics of space**.

## Core Theoretical Frameworks

### Neighborhood Effects
- **Wilson (1987) concentration effects**: spatial concentration of poverty and its self-reinforcing mechanisms.
- **Neighborhood effects literature**: Galster (2012) taxonomy — social-interactive, environmental, geographic, institutional mechanisms.
- **Moving to Opportunity (MTO)**: experimental evidence on neighborhood effects on children (Chetty, Hendren & Katz, 2016); limited effects on adults.
- **Exposure effects**: cumulative vs. contemporaneous neighborhood influence; duration dependence; critical periods in childhood.
- **Selection vs. treatment**: the fundamental identification challenge — do neighborhoods cause outcomes or do people sort into neighborhoods?

### Gentrification and Displacement
- **Classical theories**: rent gap (Neil Smith, 1979); consumer sovereignty / demand-side (Ley, 1986); stage models (Clay, 1979).
- **Measurement debates**: operationalizing gentrification — income change, demographic change, investment change, cultural change.
- **Displacement**: direct (eviction, rent increase) vs. indirect (exclusionary displacement, community disruption); chain displacement.
- **Critical perspectives**: gentrification as class remaking of space; planetary gentrification (Lees, Shin & López-Morales); state-led gentrification.
- **Quantitative evidence**: Freeman (2005), Vigdor (2002), Dragan, Ellen & Glied (2019) — contested evidence on displacement magnitude.

### Segregation and Spatial Inequality
- **Indices**: dissimilarity (D), isolation (P*), information theory (H), spatial versions (Reardon & O'Sullivan, 2004).
- **Mechanisms**: discrimination, preferences, income sorting, institutional practices, historical legacies (Rothstein, 2017; Massey & Denton, 1993).
- **Multi-scalar segregation**: macro (metro), meso (neighborhood), micro (block); experienced vs. residential segregation.
- **Intersectionality**: race-class-gender interactions in housing access and residential outcomes.

### Urban Morphology and Land Use
- **Built environment analysis**: density, land cover, building typology, street network connectivity.
- **Urban sprawl**: measurement (Ewing & Hamidi sprawl index); causes (auto-dependence, lot-size zoning, tax incentives); housing market consequences.
- **Urban form and housing**: compact city vs. sprawl debate; densification impacts on housing diversity and affordability.
- **Mixed-use and Transit-Oriented Development (TOD)**: walkability, 15-minute city, accessibility as urban form metric.

### GIS, Remote Sensing, and Spatial Data Science
- **GIS methods**: geocoding, spatial overlay, buffer analysis, network analysis, suitability modeling.
- **Remote sensing for housing**: satellite imagery for building detection, informal settlement mapping, roof condition assessment, green space measurement.
- **Spatial data science**: geostatistics, point pattern analysis, spatial clustering, geographically weighted methods.
- **Big geospatial data**: GPS traces, mobile phone data, social media geotags, street-level imagery (Google Street View), LiDAR.

### Housing Justice and Critical Geography
- **Right to the city** (Lefebvre, Harvey): housing as a social right; urban commons; spatial justice.
- **Racial capitalism**: how racial categorization structures housing markets and property value (Robinson; Taylor, 2019).
- **Financialization critique**: housing as commodity vs. home; extraction of value from place (Aalbers, Fields, Christophers).
- **Feminist geography of housing**: gendered dimensions of homeownership, domestic space, homelessness, care work and housing.
- **Postcolonial and Global South perspectives**: informal housing, land tenure, slum upgrading, colonial land regimes, Southern urbanisms.
- **Domination geography**: spatial fix, links between capitalism and spaces (David Harvey).

## Vocabulary Guide

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Place | A location imbued with meaning, identity, and social relations — not merely coordinates |
| Spatial fix | Harvey's concept: capitalism's tendency to resolve crises through geographic expansion and restructuring |
| Scale | The level of geographic resolution (body, household, neighborhood, city, region, nation, globe) and the politics of choosing it |
| Territoriality | The strategies by which individuals and groups claim, bound, and control geographic space |
| Uneven development | Systematic geographic inequality produced by capitalist accumulation processes |
| Rent gap | Difference between current capitalized ground rent and potential ground rent under highest-and-best use (Smith) |
| Displacement | Involuntary relocation from a neighborhood due to forces beyond a household's control |
| Spatial mismatch | Geographic disconnection between where low-income/minority residents live and where jobs are located (Kain, 1968) |
| Exposure | Degree to which a group's residential experience brings them into contact with another group |
| Walkability | Accessibility of destinations on foot; often measured via Walk Score or similar indices |
| Socio-spatial | Emphasizing the mutual constitution of social processes and spatial forms |
| Lived experience | First-person subjective experience of place, housing, and neighborhood — central to qualitative inquiry |

## What to Emphasize in Summaries

1. **Place specificity**: Where is the study set? How does the particular geographic context shape findings? Are results generalizable?
2. **Scale and boundary choices**: What spatial units are used? Is the MAUP acknowledged? Are boundaries endogenous to the process studied?
3. **Positionality and epistemology**: For qualitative/critical work — what is the author's standpoint? What knowledge claims are made and on what basis?
4. **Mixed methods**: If the paper combines quantitative and qualitative approaches, how are they integrated? Does one dominate?
5. **Power and politics**: Who benefits, who is harmed? What power relations shape the housing processes described?
6. **Temporal depth**: Does the paper historicize its analysis? Are path dependencies and legacies (e.g., redlining, colonial land policy) considered?
